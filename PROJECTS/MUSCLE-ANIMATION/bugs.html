// entry 01 Fixed

It learned to fly.

Every other entry on this page is some flavour of "it fell over". This one is the exact opposite, which is exactly why it's my favourite.

First, joint welding. When a leg is folded hard against its joint stop and a muscle is still pushing into it, the solver treats that joint as rigid for the substep. This simplified the solver a lot, and it helped keep things stable during the early stages of building it. Good idea.

The bug. Welding only happens inside the constraint solver. The main dynamics pass never welds. So the solver works out impulses for a stiff creature and then applies them to the real, floppy one — and those don't add up to zero momentum. On the ground, the floor soaks up the difference and nobody notices. In mid-air, every force is internal, and the error becomes thrust. That's my best explanation, and the numbers below back it hard.

The motive. I wanted the creature to stand as tall as it possibly could, so I pushed the height target higher than it could ever reach. Maybe I underestimated the learning process, haha.

I ASKED FOR STANDING

That morning, I saw that the policy was reproducing the default standing pose nicely, but it was struggling with the height it reached. So I thought: maybe I can just set every other reward to near zero and push height really high.

Going back to my little run. First thing: a very good learning curve. It had successfully learned. I was very happy, so I launched the editor to see what it actually looked like... It was flying. More specifically, it would get on its feet, jump, then crawl through the air. I was stunned, and a little proud of it. Then came the debugging.

First hypothesis. Being in the air massively narrowed down the possible sources of this infinite energy. After some value debugging, I found something that looked like the root cause: the welding.

Fitted vertical acceleration while airborne. Anything that isn't −200 is physics lying.
Switched off cm/s²
Nothing, as shipped +457
Joint-limit rows (back to the old clamp) +537
Old per-row solver instead +359
Limb self-collision +292
Joint welding −15
Welding, limit rows and limb collision together −228

Welding off takes it from climbing to barely hovering. Welding was supposed to be a solution, and it had become the problem.

Status: fixed. Welding is gone from the solver. And because a bug like this never shows up as a crash, I also wrote a test for exactly this situation: the real rig, a kilometre up, full-strength random torques, and its momentum is only allowed to change by gravity.

Looked like
A monster jumping. Then crawling through the air.
Actually was
Joint welding applied in the constraint solve but not in the dynamics, leaking momentum — plus a height target above its real standing height.
Fix
Welding removed from the solver.
Lesson
A policy is the most thorough fuzzer you will ever own. If the physics has a hole, training is how you find out.
// entry 02 Fixed

It got paid to crouch.

This one looked like success. After a long stretch of runs ending in a heap, the creature started surviving the full 30-second episode — 99% of the time, against 101 steps for random torque babble and 610 for a CEM-optimised constant controller. Great. Except it wasn't standing. It was crouching, about 300 units tall where standing is 414.

The culprit was a safety net. A blow-up guard clamped total reward to 10 per step, so a diverging simulation couldn't flood training with enormous numbers. Sensible. But the height term was scaled by 20, so every single living step hit the cap. Measured afterwards: 9.99 reward per step, all run long. Standing tall earned exactly as much as crouching. Height had become mathematically invisible.

So the policy optimised the one thing it could still see — not falling over — and did that superbly. It wasn't being lazy. It was being correct, about a reward that was wrong.

~300 UNITS TALL · STANDING IS 414 · PAID THE SAME EITHER WAY

After the fix, under normal gravity, runs hold about 365 — right on the 370 target I'd set. Which is what made me raise the target. Which is how we got entry 01.

Looked like
A creature that had finally learned to stand.
Actually was
A reward clamp sitting below the height term's scale, so every step paid the same.
Fix
Height now ramps from a minimum to a good height instead of saturating, there's a reward for keeping limbs on the ground, and the clamp scales with the configured maximum.
Lesson
Check what a number is actually measuring before celebrating it. Episode length measured survival. I wanted standing.
// entry 03 Fixed

The constant with an expiry date.

The contact solver used to have a relaxation-iteration count. I tuned it carefully, landed on 8, and moved on. The very next day, the order of the physics step changed. Nobody re-checked the 8.

So it kept doing its job under assumptions that no longer existed: the creature sank 7.5 cm into the ground at rest, and the contact forces flickered. When I finally swept it again, the right answer had become zero. At 0, resting penetration is 0.54 cm — which is just the 0.5 cm contact slop doing what it's supposed to — and contact-force flicker dropped by 96%.

And a setting whose best value is zero is a setting you don't need. It's gone from the solver now.

Looked like
Slightly mushy feet. Very easy to ignore.
Actually was
A tuned value outliving the step ordering it was tuned for.
Fix
Relax iterations 8 → 0, confirmed with a sweep. Then removed entirely.
Lesson
Every tuned constant has an expiry date, stamped on the assumptions it was tuned under.
// entry 04 Fixed

The NaN that got away.

Fall detection compared the creature's state against a threshold. NaN compares false against everything. So a creature whose simulation had completely diverged sailed straight past the check, was declared perfectly fine, and training carried on learning from it.

Nothing crashed. Nothing warned. It just quietly learned from nonsense.

Looked like
Training that made no sense, for no visible reason.
Actually was
A diverged sim passing fall detection, because every comparison against NaN is false.
Lesson
A check that can't fail isn't a check.
// entry 05 Fixed

Centimetres.

Unreal thinks in centimetres. Every physics paper I was working from thinks in metres. Guess how that goes.

(For the record: a length off by 100 is bad. Rotational inertia goes as length squared, so that one's off by ten thousand — and it still produces motion that looks like something.)

Looked like
Everything subtly, confidently wrong.
Actually was
Centimetres meeting metres.
Lesson
Pick one unit, and convert exactly once, at the boundary.
// entry 06 Fixed

Ghosts from the last episode.

Two smaller ones to round it out. State surviving a reset, so an episode started out carrying something left over from the one before it. And joint conventions that were wrong in a way that still looked plausible — which is the most dangerous kind of wrong, because nothing about it looks broken.

Looked like
Nothing. That was the problem.
Actually was
Stale state between episodes, and joint conventions that were plausibly wrong.
Lesson
Plausible is not correct. Physics is extremely good at plausible.
// entry 07 Fixed

It stood up. It would not stop shaking.

This is the good news entry, and it still took a fix. The creature finally got to full height — past 414, the whole thing, on the ground, on purpose. And it stood there vibrating like a washing machine on a bad floor.

Nothing was wrong, exactly. The policy outputs 68 muscle activations every control tick, and I was feeding those numbers straight into the solver as-is. A muscle could go from slack to full clench and back inside two ticks, because nothing in the system said it couldn't. Real muscle can't do that. Mine had no idea it wasn't allowed to.

Worse, that's a perfectly good way to balance. Buzzing at 60 Hz is a stable strategy: over-correct, over-correct back, never actually fall. The reward was paying for height and for staying upright, and shaking delivered both. So it shook.

BEFORE / AFTER · SAME HEIGHT · ONE OF THEM IS WATCHABLE

The fix came from both ends at once, which is why it worked. On the physics side, muscle activation is now smoothed on its way into the solver instead of using the raw policy output — a muscle ramps toward what it's told rather than teleporting there. On the reward side, rapid changes in muscle intensity now cost something, so the policy is charged for the thing it was previously getting for free.

Either one alone half-works. Smoothing without the penalty just means the policy shakes harder to push the same jitter through the filter. The penalty without smoothing leaves the physics able to accept impossible inputs in the first place. Together, it stands still.

Looked like
The milestone I'd been chasing, having a seizure.
Actually was
Raw per-tick activations going straight to the solver, and nothing in the reward objecting to a muscle slamming between slack and full every frame.
Fix
Muscle activation is smoothed before it reaches the solver, and the reward penalises rapid changes in muscle intensity.
Lesson
If the physics will accept something a real body can't do, the policy will find it and build its whole strategy on it. Constrain the simulation, then pay for the behaviour you want — one without the other just moves the problem.
// what all of this changed

I stopped nudging parameters.

What actually changed, after all of the above, was how I debug. I used to nudge parameters until it looked better. Now I write down what I think is wrong, work out what test would distinguish it from the alternatives, run that, and only then touch the code.

The flying bug is the cleanest example of it. "It looks like a jump" was a perfectly reasonable reading of the video. "Airborne acceleration must equal gravity" is not a reading. It's a test, and it doesn't care what the video looks like.

Slower per attempt. Enormously faster overall. And a lot of those distinguishing tests stick around afterwards, which is how the project ended up with a test suite that checks physics invariants instead of my eyesight.
