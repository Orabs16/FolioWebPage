// 02 · why bother

What if I stopped authoring the movement?

Hand animation is boring. Not the result — the process. You decide, frame by frame, exactly what a body does, and the body never once surprises you. You already know how it ends, because you're the one typing it.

So I wanted to find out what happens if I stop deciding. Give a creature muscles, give it physics, and let it work the rest out on its own.

That turned out to need three things I didn't have: a physics solver fast enough to train against, a way to author muscles onto a skeletal mesh, and an RL setup that doesn't fall apart. So I built all three.

The first goal is deliberately unglamorous: stand up. Not walk, not run — just stay upright. Everything below is what it took to get to the point where that's even a fair question to ask.

// 04 · giving it muscles

Turning a skeletal mesh into a physical creature.

A skeletal mesh knows where its bones are. It has no idea what any of them weigh, or how hard they can pull. My solver needs both, so I built an editor workflow for painting mass and muscle onto the rig.

The bit I care about: muscles use authored extension/flexion strength curves, not one constant torque value. Real muscle is weakest at the ends of its range — try holding a dumbbell with your arm fully straight versus at 90°. A single torque number throws that away and gives you a creature with the same strength in every pose, which looks exactly as wrong as it sounds.

// 05 · writing my own solver

A physics solver built for batched simulation.

I could have used the engine's constraint solver. But training means simulating the same creature thousands of times over, and a general-purpose solver spends most of its budget on generality I don't need. So I wrote a reduced-coordinate Featherstone Articulated Body Algorithm from scratch in C++.

Featherstone is one of those algorithms that feels like a magic trick the first time it works: it gets you forward dynamics in O(n) without ever building the mass matrix — three sweeps over the tree and you're done. The current topology is a floating 6-DOF root, then 3-DOF ball joints and 1-DOF yaw joints — 41 bodies, 68 degrees of freedom.

// 06 · 2048 at once

Thousands of environments. One solver.

Here's the thing about RL: it wants an obscene number of simulated seconds. Running environments one at a time is hopeless. But every environment has the identical topology — same bones, same joints, same everything — which means the work is the same instruction applied to different numbers. Which is exactly what SIMD is for.

So the hot path is AVX2, eight creatures per register, with the whole batch spread across parallel jobs. The trick itself is very cool. Working with it was, honestly, a pain — SIMD bugs don't crash. One lane quietly holds a wrong number and everything carries on as if nothing happened, so you find out a week later when the training makes no sense.

Which is why the boring scalar version never got deleted. When the two disagree, the fast one is wrong.

// 07 · the rewrite that paid off

From individual constraints to a global system.

My first constraint solver went row by row, Gauss-Seidel style. It worked, in the sense that it produced numbers. It also meant every constraint was solved in ignorance of the others, and a foot on the ground had no idea a knee was at its limit.

So I threw it out and assembled the whole thing globally — ground contacts, joint limits and limb self-collisions in one system, solved with Projected SOR. The neat part is that I never have to form the mass matrix to build it: I already had an impulse-response query lying around from the ABA, and that's exactly what each entry of A = JM⁻¹Jᵀ + R needs.

CONTACT Ground Contact constraints
LIMIT Joints Joint-limit constraints
COLLISION Limbs Self-collision constraints
→
A = JM⁻¹Jᵀ + R Global system Projected SOR

I expected the rewrite to cost me performance in exchange for correctness. Profiler said 1.57× cheaper than the per-row version. I'll take it.

// 08 · letting it learn

Learning to stay upright before learning to walk.

The learning layer uses Unreal Learning Agents with PPO. The policy receives information about the creature's physical state and produces a normalized action for every controllable degree of freedom.

Observations: torso orientation and velocity, height error, joint positions and velocities, contacts. The reward says stay alive, stay upright, stay balanced, and stop flailing — that last one is a torque penalty, and without it you get a creature that solves standing by vibrating.

No locomotion in the objective. None. It is very tempting to skip ahead to walking, and every time I'm tempted, the creature plants its face into the ground and reminds me that I can't yet prove it can even hold still.

// 09 · watching it learn

Debugging from raw numbers is a nightmare.

Training gives you a column of floats. Reward up, reward down. Working out why from that is genuinely awful — you end up staring at a log file trying to imagine a creature, and you are always imagining the wrong one.

So I built an in-engine tool to just watch the thing. Half of what looked like a mysterious reward collapse turned out to be completely obvious the second I could see the creature doing something stupid.

TRAINING / SIMULATION TOOL

Training runs on a background thread, because the Python PPO round-trip will happily freeze the whole Unreal editor otherwise. Then the visualiser nearly reintroduced the exact same stall by reaching into the live policy every frame — so it takes periodic snapshots instead. Watching the creature shouldn't be allowed to slow down the creature.

// 10 · proving it is not lying to me

Every fix used to quietly break two older ones.

This was the loop I kept falling into: change something, fix the thing in front of me, silently break something I'd already fixed last week, and only notice much later once three of those had stacked up and the whole thing was on fire. Then I get to work out which of the last twenty changes did it. Not fun.

A physics system makes that worse, because a broken change still produces motion that looks perfectly convincing. Your eyes will cheerfully sign off on nonsense. So now there are tests, they run on their own, and I find out I've made a mistake in seconds instead of a fortnight.

The one I'm proudest of is invariant testing. Angular momentum has to be conserved whether or not the result looks nice, so I assert on that rather than on my own judgement — and it has caught things no tolerance check would ever have flagged.

// 11 · things that ate whole days

A field guide to "it fell over again".

Every single bug in this project presents identically: the creature falls over. That's the whole error message. Here are the ones that cost me the most.

The forward kinematics one. I was dropping the bone's rotation offset relative to its parent. The creature spawned rotated wrong, so I "fixed" its root orientation. Then its height looked wrong, so I "fixed" that too. Two wrong assumptions stacked on one real bug, and by the time I found the actual cause I had to unpick both of them. Lesson filed permanently.

The NaN that got away. Fall detection compared against a threshold. NaN compares false against everything, so a diverged creature sailed straight past the check and kept training on garbage. Nothing crashed. It just quietly learned from nonsense.

The units. Unreal thinks in centimetres. Every physics paper I was working from thinks in metres. Guess how that goes.

Stale state surviving a reset, and joint conventions that were wrong in a way that still looked plausible — those two rounded it out.

What actually changed, after all that, was how I debug. I stopped nudging parameters until it looked better. Now I write down what I think is wrong, work out what test would distinguish it from the alternatives, run that, and only then touch the code. Slower per attempt. Enormously faster overall.

// 14 · what this taught me

The solver was the easy part.

Featherstone is written down in books. You can look it up. What nobody writes down is that the same system has to be correct mathematically, plausible physically, fast enough to train against, and configurable by someone who has no intention of reading your C++. Getting all four at once is the actual job.

The other thing I got out of this is a debugging habit I didn't have before, described above. It came from losing enough days to confident guesses that I stopped making them.
