// 01 · giving it muscles

Turning a skeletal mesh into a physical creature.

A skeletal mesh knows where its bones are. It has no idea what any of them weigh, or how hard they can pull. My solver needs both, so I built an editor workflow for painting mass and muscle onto the rig.

The bit I care about: muscles use authored extension/flexion strength curves, not one constant torque value. Real muscle is weakest at the ends of its range — try holding a dumbbell with your arm fully straight versus at 90°. A single torque number throws that away and gives you a creature with the same strength in every pose, which looks exactly as wrong as it sounds.

// 02 · writing my own solver

A physics solver built for batched simulation.

I could have used the engine's constraint solver. But training means simulating the same creature an enormous number of times, and a general-purpose solver spends most of its budget on generality I don't need. So I wrote a reduced-coordinate Featherstone Articulated Body Algorithm from scratch in C++.

Featherstone is one of those algorithms that feels like a magic trick the first time it works: it gets you forward dynamics in O(n) without ever building the mass matrix — three sweeps over the tree and you're done. The topology is a floating 6-DOF root, then 3-DOF ball joints and 1-DOF yaw joints — 41 bodies, 68 degrees of freedom.

01

Reduced coordinates

The body is described by its joint angles, not by 41 free-floating pieces held together with constraints. Nothing can drift apart, because there is nothing to drift.

02

Featherstone ABA

O(n) forward dynamics without ever building the full mass matrix. The 52-bone rig collapses to a 41-body tree: the skeleton's bone count and the solver's topology are two different things.

03

Data-oriented state

Structure-of-Arrays all the way down, laid out for the one thing it has to do: process many identical creatures at once.

// 03 · many at once

Many environments. One solver.

Here's the thing about RL: it wants an obscene number of simulated seconds. Running environments one at a time is hopeless. But every environment has the identical topology — same bones, same joints, same everything — which means the work is the same instruction applied to different numbers. Which is exactly what SIMD is for.

So the hot path is AVX2, eight creatures per register, with the whole batch spread across parallel jobs. The trick itself is very cool. Working with it was, honestly, a pain — SIMD bugs don't crash. One lane quietly holds a wrong number and everything carries on as if nothing happened, so you find out a week later when the training makes no sense.

Which is why the boring scalar version never got deleted. When the two disagree, the fast one is wrong.

The batch size that actually trains is 64 — not the biggest number the solver can survive. I measured it: going to 256 buys about 21% more samples per second, but that's more than my laptop can sustain reliably over time, and it costs four times the iteration latency. Bigger looked better; in reality, it isn't.

// 04 · the rewrite that paid off

From individual constraints to a global system.

My first constraint solver went row by row, Gauss-Seidel style. It worked, in the sense that it produced numbers. It also meant every constraint was solved in ignorance of the others, and a foot on the ground had no idea a knee was at its limit.

So I threw it out and assembled the whole thing globally — ground contacts, joint limits and limb self-collisions in one system, solved with Projected SOR. The neat part is that I never have to form the mass matrix to build it: I already had an impulse-response query lying around from the ABA, and that's exactly what each entry of A = JM⁻¹Jᵀ + R needs. That's the equation. Told you I was proud of it.

CONTACT Ground 41 contact points
LIMIT Joints Joint-limit constraints
COLLISION Limbs Self-collision constraints
→
A = JM⁻¹Jᵀ + R Global system Projected SOR

I expected the rewrite to cost me performance in exchange for correctness. The global solve came out 1.57× cheaper than the per-row path — while doing 64 solver sweeps against the old path's 16, because a dense sweep costs no tree traversals. It also cut peak penetration by more than a third and held the torso nearly half again as high. To be clear about the claim: that's the constraint solve, not an end-to-end framerate number.

A few solver features along the way didn't survive contact with reality. They live in the bug journal, so this page can stick to what's actually running.

// 05 · letting it learn

Learning to stay upright before learning to walk.

The learning layer is my own PPO, written in PyTorch: clipped surrogate, GAE, KL early-stop, advantage normalisation, value clipping. No RL framework underneath it, which means the numbers that tell you whether PPO is actually healthy — loss, KL, entropy — are right there for me to read. If it's wrong, it's wrong in a way I can see.

It runs as a separate process. Python trains, a headless Unreal commandlet simulates, and the two talk over TCP on loopback with a small binary framed protocol.

Observations: torso orientation and velocity, height error, joint positions and velocities, contacts. The reward says reach the target height, keep your limbs on the ground, hold a sensible pose, and stop flailing — that last one is a torque penalty, and without it you get a creature that solves standing by vibrating.

Two policy architectures share the same trainer: a 256×256 MLP, and a pure-PyTorch Mamba with recurrent PPO. The recurrent one is meaningfully faster early — roughly 20–30% fewer samples to reach the first survival milestones — and then the two converge once the episode cap starts to bind.

// 06 · watching it learn

Debugging from raw numbers is a nightmare.

Training gives you a column of floats. Reward up, reward down. Working out why from that is genuinely awful — you end up staring at a log file trying to imagine a creature, and you are always imagining the wrong one.

So I built an in-engine tool to just watch the thing. Half of what looked like a mysterious reward collapse turned out to be completely obvious the second I could see the creature doing something stupid.

TRAINING / SIMULATION TOOL

Training runs entirely outside the editor. The editor panel is a remote control: it launches the headless run, tails its log, draws the training curves, and can stop it. Close the editor and training carries on. The panel even writes out a .bat that reproduces the identical run with no editor involved.

TO FILMThe training panel: launch a run, watch the curves draw live, hit Stop. About 20 seconds.

THE EDITOR IS A REMOTE CONTROL, NOT THE TRAINER

Each run pins its own config to disk, and two hashes stop me lying to myself: one covers the observation layout and the rig, the other the full physics and reward setup. A checkpoint that doesn't match refuses to resume. I added those after losing time to a run that was quietly training against settings I'd since changed.

And any finished run can come straight back into the engine: pick it in the panel, hit Play Run, and it exports and plays in the viewport — MLP or Mamba, running in C++. The MLP path has a test asserting C++ and PyTorch agree to 1e-4. The Mamba executor is brand new; an offline replay of a real export matched PyTorch to 1.2e-7, and the in-engine version still has to prove the same. Otherwise I'd be watching a subtly different creature from the one that trained, and never know it.

// 07 · proving it is not lying to me

Every fix used to quietly break two older ones.

This was the loop I kept falling into: change something, fix the thing in front of me, silently break something I'd already fixed last week, and only notice much later once three of those had stacked up and the whole thing was on fire. Then I get to work out which of the last twenty changes did it. Not fun.

A physics system makes that worse, because a broken change still produces motion that looks perfectly convincing. Your eyes will cheerfully sign off on nonsense. So now there are tests, they run on their own, and I find out I've made a mistake in seconds instead of a fortnight.

The ones I trust most are invariant tests. Momentum has to be conserved whether or not the result looks nice, so I assert on that rather than on my own judgement. One of them hangs the real rig a kilometre up, hits it with full-strength random torques, and checks that its momentum changes by exactly gravity and nothing else.

The solver also has to pass a stability gate before anything trains on it: 50 seconds of continuous ground contact with all 41 bodies, under a passive drop and under random torque babble, with no divergence and no NaNs. That sounds unimpressive until you've watched a solver explode at second three for a fortnight. It is also, as the creature later demonstrated, not at all the same thing as the physics being right.
